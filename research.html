Why Didn’t Video Kill the Radio Star? Television Entry, Competition, and Adjustment in Radio Markets

How do firms adapt when a new technology threatens their business? I study how U.S. radio stations responded to the arrival of television, combining newly assembled station financial records with differences in television access created by the FCC’s 1948–1952 freeze on television authorizations. Revenue from major radio networks fell by 31.5 percent and local advertising revenue rose by 12.5 percent, relative to their pre-television averages. I find no evidence of a decline in total revenue. These changes were concentrated among stations that had earned network revenue before television arrived, with the most dependent stations suffering the largest losses in that source of revenue. Competition among radio stations also mattered. Within the same markets, stations with prior network revenue lost ground in local advertising relative to rivals that had earned none. The dollar gap was larger where more radio stations competed. A simple model of airtime allocation explains how changes in marginal revenue shift radio time toward less affected uses. This reallocation can cushion revenue losses, while continued operation depends on whether revenue covers fixed operating costs. Together, the findings show how technological change can reorganize firms’ sources of revenue, with adjustment shaped by their earlier business models and the competition they face.

Spatial Diffusion of Unions: Historical Evidence from the UK (with Keitaro Ninomiya)

Did workplace accidents lead workers to organize, and did unionization then spread along the railway network? Using newly digitized records from 19th-century British railways, this project examines whether accident exposure predicted the formation of railway unions and whether union activity diffused across space through the rail lines connecting workplaces. The analysis speaks to how labor organization emerges after workplace risk and how it travels through the networks that link workers across locations.

Special Economic Zones and Local Economic Development in India

Do special economic zones promote local development, and how far do their effects extend? I study India’s Special Economic Zones using village-level nighttime lights to measure economic activity, alongside census data on firms and employment. I compare changes across villages at different distances from zones and account for overlapping exposure using each zone’s size and distance. Nighttime lights increase most strongly within 5 km of a zone, with effects declining sharply farther away. Manufacturing employment and firm counts also rise in nearby villages. The findings suggest that SEZs can stimulate local development, with gains concentrated in their immediate surroundings.

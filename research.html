Why Didn’t Video Kill the Radio Star? Television Entry, Competition, and Adjustment in Radio Markets

Abstract

New technology can change where a firm makes its money, even when its total revenue stays about the same. I study how U.S. radio stations responded to the arrival of television. I use newly collected FCC financial records and differences in when markets got television because of the FCC’s 1948–1952 licensing freeze. Television reduced revenue from major radio networks by 31.5 percent and increased local advertising revenue by 12.5 percent, compared with their averages before television. Total revenue did not change significantly. These changes were concentrated among stations that earned network revenue before television arrived. Stations that relied more heavily on networks lost the most network revenue, while stations without network revenue showed little change. Competition also mattered. Highly network-dependent stations lost more network revenue in markets with more radio stations. Network stations also gained the least in local advertising where they competed with stations that had no network revenue. A simple model shows how stations can shift airtime toward other uses when television makes network programming less profitable. These results suggest that a station’s response to television varied with its earlier revenue sources and with the competition it faced.

Spatial Diffusion of Unions: Historical Evidence from the UK (with Keitaro Ninomiya)

Abstract

Why do workers form unions, and how does unionization spread? This project examines the rise of organized railway labor in nineteenth-century Britain, focusing on the early years of one of the country’s first nationwide railway unions. Using newly digitized records of workplace accidents and railway union activity, we study whether accidents encouraged workers to organize and whether unionization spread between workplaces connected by railway lines. The project sheds light on how workplace risks can lead to labor organization and how connections between workers help early union movements grow.

Special Economic Zones and Local Economic Development in India

Abstract

Can relatively small special economic zones promote local development, and how far do their effects extend? While much of the existing literature focuses on large economic zones, India’s SEZs are typically much smaller. I study whether these smaller zones can still generate economic gains beyond their boundaries. Using village-level nighttime lights to measure economic activity, alongside census data on firms and employment, I compare changes across villages at different distances from SEZs. I also account for exposure to multiple zones based on their size and distance. Nighttime lights increase most strongly within 5 km of a zone, with effects declining sharply farther away. Manufacturing employment and firm counts also rise in nearby villages. The findings show that even relatively small SEZs can stimulate local development, although the benefits are concentrated close to the zones themselves.
